# Society Systems

## Principle

Society should emerge from repeated individual actions, but the engine still needs mechanisms for persistent social structures.

Institutions are not mystical global controllers. They are shared records, roles, expectations, enforcement practices, and beliefs maintained by people.

## Families and households

Support:

- Pair bonds or culturally defined partnerships.
- Biological and adoptive parenthood.
- Childcare.
- Inheritance.
- Household food sharing.
- Division of labor.
- Kin obligations.
- Family reputation.
- Separation and remarriage.
- Orphan care.

Children should learn language, norms, and skills primarily through actual interaction.

## Tribes, communities, and factions

A person can belong to overlapping groups:

- Household.
- Clan.
- Tribe.
- Settlement.
- Profession.
- Religion.
- Political faction.
- Secret society.
- Military unit.

Membership influences trust priors, obligations, identity, language exposure, and prestige.

## Settlements

A settlement may contain:

- Residents.
- Visitors.
- Buildings.
- Resource claims.
- Storage.
- Workplaces.
- Markets.
- leadership roles.
- norms and laws.
- defense.
- religious sites.
- infrastructure.

Settlement-level summaries are indexes and records, not replacements for residents.

## Occupations

Occupations develop through:

- Skill.
- Need.
- apprenticeship.
- family tradition.
- opportunity.
- prestige.
- coercion.
- market demand.
- cultural restrictions.

Professional vocabulary can emerge from repeated occupation-specific communication.

## Ownership

Ownership is a belief-backed social institution.

It may involve:

- Personal possession.
- Household property.
- Communal property.
- State or temple ownership.
- Customary access.
- Contested claims.

An ownership record is meaningful only because people recognize or enforce it.

## Trade

Trade should include:

- Wants and inventories.
- perceived value.
- trust.
- debt.
- promises.
- bargaining.
- transport cost.
- risk.
- communication uncertainty.
- cultural norms.
- currency or barter.
- fraud.
- reputation.

Cross-language trade is a major driver of bilingualism and pidgins.

## Conflict

Conflict may emerge from:

- Resource scarcity.
- revenge.
- fear.
- ownership disputes.
- misunderstanding.
- status competition.
- ideology.
- migration.
- leadership.
- inherited hostility.

Combatants remain individuals with fear, loyalty, injuries, beliefs, and communication limits.

## Migration

Migration utility may include:

- Food security.
- safety.
- land.
- kin.
- employment.
- climate.
- persecution.
- trade access.
- marriage.
- rumor.
- language compatibility.

Migration carries language and culture.

## Leadership and prestige

Prestige may derive from:

- Skill.
- wealth.
- generosity.
- lineage.
- military success.
- age.
- religious role.
- knowledge.
- charisma.
- coercive power.

Prestigious speakers can accelerate language adoption, but influence should remain socially contextual.

## Norms and law

Norms emerge from:

- Repetition.
- teaching.
- punishment.
- reputation.
- authority.
- shared belief.
- religion.
- practical coordination.

Agents can:

- Follow.
- violate.
- reinterpret.
- contest.
- enforce.
- hide violations.
- selectively apply norms.

## Religion and ideology

Potential systems:

- Ritual.
- sacred places.
- taboo.
- priesthood.
- myth transmission.
- moral rules.
- conversion.
- schism.
- religious vocabulary.
- political legitimacy.

These should be implemented after the basic social and communication systems work.

## Writing and administration

Later systems may include:

- Marks tied to concepts.
- accounting.
- ownership records.
- law.
- correspondence.
- religious text.
- bureaucracy.
- schooling.
- standardization.
- literacy differences.

Written signals remain observable forms and still require learned interpretation.
